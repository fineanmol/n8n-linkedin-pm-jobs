Customer Feedback Automation (2-minute STAR)
Situation

One operational challenge we faced was that customer feedback was coming from multiple sources—emails, customer success calls, support tickets, and feedback forms. Product managers were manually consolidating all this information before backlog refinement, which made prioritization slow and inconsistent.

Task

My goal was to create a more structured feedback workflow so that customer issues could automatically reach the right teams while reducing manual effort and improving how we prioritized product improvements.

Action

I first mapped the existing process with Customer Success, Support, and Engineering to identify bottlenecks. We then designed a structured feedback form where customers selected categories like Bug, Feature Request, Process Improvement, or Usability, along with mandatory details such as affected module and business impact.

I worked with Engineering to define the business rules. Based on the selected category, the backend automatically routed the feedback. For example, bugs created Jira bug tickets, feature requests entered the product review backlog, process improvement requests were routed for operational review, and support issues went directly to Customer Success. The integration used Jira REST APIs so tickets were created with all the required information, reducing manual work and ensuring Engineering received standardized requests.

Result

This eliminated manual ticket creation, improved the quality and consistency of backlog items, reduced duplicate requests, and allowed us to prioritize features using structured customer insights rather than scattered conversations.

Lesson

The biggest lesson for me was that automation isn't just about saving time. It's about creating consistent processes that improve decision-making and allow teams to focus on solving customer problems instead of managing administrative work.

1. Cybersecurity onboarding failure story (Customized for CRP)
   Question:
   Tell me about a product failure or a time an assumption proved wrong.
   Situation
   One initiative we worked on in our Cyber Risk Profile platform was improving the onboarding experience for enterprise customers.
   The platform required organizations to configure multiple security settings before they could start their first cyber risk assessment. This included defining organizational assets, selecting assessment templates, integrating security tools, assigning user roles, and configuring compliance requirements.
   Because different organizations had different security policies, our initial assumption was that giving customers complete flexibility during onboarding would provide the best experience.

Task
Our objective was to reduce onboarding dependency on Customer Success while allowing organizations to configure the platform according to their own security environment.

Action
After launch, we closely monitored onboarding analytics using Google Analytics and collected feedback through Customer Success and implementation teams.
The data showed that many customers were abandoning onboarding before completing their first assessment.
Support requests also increased because customers were unsure which security configurations were actually required.
When we interviewed customers, we discovered something interesting.
Most organizations didn't want to configure every security option immediately.
They wanted to complete their first cyber risk assessment as quickly as possible and th gradually customize the platform once they understood its capabilities.
Based on these insights, I proposed redesigning the onboarding flow.
Instead of exposing every configuration option upfront, we introduced:
Recommended security templates based on organization size
Default security policies
Step-by-step guided onboarding
Progressive configuration
Advanced settings only after the first successful assessment
Before rolling it out completely, we validated the redesigned onboarding through A/B testing.

Result
The simplified onboarding significantly improved completion rates.
Customers reached their first cyber risk assessment much faster.
Customer Success received fewer onboarding-related support requests.
Organizations started seeing product value earlier, which also improved feature adoption after onboarding.

Lesson
The biggest lesson I learned was that enterprise customers value clarity more than flexibility during their first interaction with a product.
As Product Managers, we often assume more customization creates more value.
In reality, customers first want confidence, then flexibility.
Since then, whenever I design workflows, I always ask:
"What's the minimum information users need to become successful?"
rather than
"How many options can we provide?"

Any campaign failed- lesson learned-

One important lesson I learned was during the launch of a customized onboarding feature for a B2B CRM platform used by infrastructure and construction-focused clients.

Initially, we assumed that customers would highly value maximum flexibility and deep customization during onboarding. Based on that assumption, we positioned the feature heavily around customization capabilities and also ran awareness campaigns to increase adoption.

However, after the launch, we noticed that apart from a few early adopters, the adoption rate did not increase significantly. At the same time, support tickets increased, onboarding took longer, and many users seemed overwhelmed by too many options and workflows.

To understand the issue better, I worked closely with customer-facing teams and analyzed customer feedback, onboarding funnel data, and user behavior patterns. What we realized was that customers valued simplicity and faster activation more than extensive customization in the initial stage.

Based on those insights, we changed our approach. Instead of promoting flexibility first, we introduced standardized onboarding templates and positioned them as faster and easier solutions for operational teams. Advanced customization options were then provided later, once users were more comfortable with the platform.

After implementing these changes, onboarding became smoother, adoption rates improved, and support dependency reduced significantly.

The biggest lesson I learned from that experience was that in B2B environments, especially with technically complex products, more features do not always create more value. Sometimes simplicity, clarity, and reducing customer effort are far more important for adoption and customer satisfaction.

It also reinforced the importance of continuously listening to customer feedback and aligning product communication and positioning with actual customer behavior rather than internal assumptions.”
